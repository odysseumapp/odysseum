The night before he left for the mainland, Aldous Vale spread a sheet of paper across the kitchen table and asked his daughter to draw the island.

Mara was seven. She drew a circle.

“That’s a plate,” he said.

“It’s Bellwether.”

“Bellwether has a spine.” He took her hand, pencil and all, and guided it into a long ridge that ran north to south. “And a crooked shoulder here, where the light stands. And a harbor that faces the wrong way, so the wind never quite forgives it.”

Outside, the real island breathed in the dark. The lamp turned. Somewhere down the hill the bell in the tower rang once, for no reason anyone could name.

“Now the road,” he said.

She drew the road from the harbor up to the white house and stopped, because that was where the road stopped.

“Good. Now the rule.” He waited until she looked up. “A map is a promise you make to a stranger. You’re telling them: *this is where things are.* So you only draw what you’re sure of, and you leave the rest blank, and you never, ever pretend the blank is the sea.”

“What if it moves?”

He laughed. Then he stopped laughing, and looked at the window for a while.

“Then you draw it again,” he said. “Every time.”

He gave her the pencil. It was already too short to hold properly. She kept it anyway.
