The letter arrived on a Tuesday, folded into the shape of a small, imperfect square.

Mara almost threw it away.

It had come between a gas bill and an invitation to a wedding she did not intend to attend. There was no return address, no stamp she recognized, only her name written in a hand she had spent twelve years trying to forget.

Outside, the city was doing what it always did when it rained: pretending not to notice. Buses sighed against the curb. Someone argued with a taxi. In the flat upstairs, a piano found the same wrong note for the fourth time.

She put the kettle on.

This was the sort of thing her mother would have done. Faced with impossible news, or a broken heart, or a visitor who had arrived without warning, her mother had always started with the kettle. As though there were nothing in the world that could not wait three minutes.

Mara turned the envelope over.

On the back, pressed into the paper so lightly she nearly missed it, was a tiny drawing of a lighthouse. Three windows. A crooked door. The same lighthouse her father had drawn in the margins of every map he ever made.

*Not possible,* she thought.

But her hands were already opening it.

---

There were only seven words.

**The island is not where we left it.**

Beneath them, a set of coordinates. Beneath those, a date: tomorrow.

The kettle clicked off.

For a long time Mara stood in the narrow kitchen, listening to the rain find its way through the open window. She thought of the island. Of the white house above the harbor. Of a boat pulling away from the jetty and her father standing at its edge, growing smaller, one hand raised.

She had always remembered him waving goodbye.

Now she wondered if he had been asking her to wait.

Upstairs, the pianist finally found the right note. It hung in the air between them, uncertain and beautiful, and then was gone.

Mara took her old atlas down from the shelf.

The page where Bellwether should have been was blank.
