Elin opened the door before Mara knocked.

“You have his eyes,” she said. “Come in. There’s soup.”

The lighthouse smelled of beeswax and onions. Boots stood in a patient row beside the door. From somewhere above came the slow turning sound of machinery that had never learned to rest.

Mara followed Elin into a kitchen full of afternoon light.

“I got a letter.”

“I thought you might.”

“From my father.”

Elin set two bowls on the table. Her hands were steady. That was what Mara would remember afterward: how steady they were.

“Eat first.”

On the windowsill stood a photograph in a silver frame. Three people in front of the lighthouse. Elin, much younger. A man Mara did not recognize.

And herself, at an age she had no memory of being here.

She picked up the photograph.

Elin turned from the stove.

“Oh,” she said quietly. “I meant to put that away.”
