“You were four,” Elin said.

She took the frame gently from Mara’s hands and set it face down on the windowsill, as though it might be listening.

“Your mother brought you up here the morning he went. She had a feeling. Ines always had feelings, and she was usually right, which is a hard way to live.”

“I don’t remember any of this.”

“No. You wouldn’t.”

The machinery above them turned. The soup cooled. Elin sat down across the table and folded her steady hands.

“The island keeps what people remember, Mara. That’s all it is. That’s all it has ever been. And when a child decides very hard that something did not happen—” she lifted one shoulder, “—the island is polite about it.”

Mara looked at the back of the frame. Cheap velvet, a wire stand, a strip of tape holding the whole thing together.

“So the bell tower.”

“You dared yourself up those stairs when you were ten. You remember that.”

“I remember it on the east side.”

“Then that’s where it is, until you remember otherwise.”

Outside, the light swung across the water and came back.

“And my father?”

Elin was quiet for a long time.

“Eat your soup,” she said at last. “Then we’ll go and look at the places you’ve forgotten. Bring the little map. It’ll want to come.”
