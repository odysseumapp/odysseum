Her father had taught her that every map was an argument.

A road said: *this is how you get there.* A border said: *this is where you stop.* Even the smallest blue line of a river insisted that the water knew which way it wanted to go.

“Never trust a map that pretends to be the truth,” he had told her.

She had been eight. She had wanted to know where the dragons went.

Now, in the yellow light of her kitchen, Mara ran her fingers over a page that had no right to be empty. She could feel the impression of the island, its long spine and crooked northern shore. The ink had vanished, but the paper remembered.

Something slipped from the binding.

A second map. No bigger than her palm. It showed a harbor, a village, a road that ended halfway up a hill.

At the bottom, in her own childhood handwriting, someone had written:

> For when you need to come back.

Mara blinked. The road was a little longer now.

She closed the atlas and held it against her chest, as though that might keep the past where it belonged. It smelled of dust and the cedar cupboard in their old house.

For the first time in twelve years, she booked a ticket home.
